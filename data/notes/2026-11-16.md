# The Record on Which History Will Judge Us, Vol. 4: A Nuremberg for Our Time

As I write this, Eighty-one years ago yesterday, on November 20, 1945, a courtroom in Nuremberg opened to try the surviving leaders of Nazi Germany. Four nations, the United States, Great Britain, the Soviet Union and France, had every reason to simply line them up against a wall. They chose a trial instead. America's chief prosecutor, Justice Robert H. Jackson, said it plainly in his opening: "That four great nations, flushed with victory and stung with injury stay the hand of vengeance and voluntarily submit their captive enemies to the judgment of the law is one of the most significant tributes that Power has ever paid to reason." Twenty-two men stood trial. On October 1, 1946, twelve were sentenced to death, seven to prison, and three were acquitted. Those acquittals matter. They are how the world knew it was justice and not revenge.

Nuremberg left us more than verdicts. It left principles, later set down by the United Nations, that America helped write: that a head of state or government official is not relieved of responsibility for a crime under international law because of the office he held, and that acting on the orders of a government or a superior is no excuse either, "provided a moral choice was in fact possible to him." Jackson knew those rules would one day be held up against the nations that wrote them. "We must never forget," he warned, "that the record on which we judge these defendants today is the record on which history will judge us tomorrow. To pass these defendants a poisoned chalice is to put it to our own lips as well."

I wish I could write as poetic as this…ICE's own records list near 30 deaths in its custody in 2026, the most on its public list, which goes back to 2018, and half again as many as the year before. In Minneapolis this January, an ICE agent shot and killed Renee Good, a 37-year-old mother of three. Seventeen days later, a Border Patrol agent shot and killed Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old ICU nurse. Then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem said he brandished a gun, but no video has shown that; bystander footage shows an agent pulling the gun from his waistband and turning away moments before the first shot. Stephen Miller and Noem reportedly demanded that ICE arrest 3,000 people a day. On Pete Hegseth's watch, the Washington Post reported, a September 2025 strike on an alleged drug boat included a second strike that killed two men clinging to the wreckage. Hegseth says he did not order that second strike; legal experts say killing survivors is unlawful, and some call it a war crime. And all the while, the president reported more than $1.4 billion from his family's crypto ventures, including World Liberty Financial, which he co-founded with his sons. We need to show the world we’re serious. Not just for our own thirst for accountability but for the world’s trust.

So here is my plea. I am not asking for vengeance. I am asking for exactly what Nuremberg offered: evidence gathered and preserved, independent prosecutors, grand juries, defense lawyers, open courtrooms and the presumption of innocence, with verdicts that may convict and may acquit. Let everyone who set these policies or carried them out, from Trump, Hegseth, Miller and Noem down to the agents in the street, and those like the president's sons who profited from their closeness to power, answer to the evidence under the law. A badge is not a shield, a title is not a pardon, and "I was following orders" was rejected as a defense at Nuremberg. We owe that much to Renee Good, to Alex Pretti, and to the dozens of people who have died in ICE custody.

And we owe it to the world. Our allies have watched us insult them, threaten them and walk away from our word, and no apology alone will repair that. Repentance has to be shown. Germany, the nation in the dock at Nuremberg, spent decades facing its record honestly and became one of our closest friends. America can do the same work. A lawful reckoning, held in the open, would tell the world that we made a grave mistake, that we know it, and that our institutions are still strong enough to correct it. A sex abuser or domestic partner abuser is not going to beat his partner and then apologize and move on. The relationship, trust and the damage is too much. You must atone and prove with actions not words that we’re serious. Eighty-one years ago, Power paid tribute to reason. I am asking every one of us to demand that our country pay that tribute again: to the families of the dead, to the allies we have wronged, and to every nation waiting to see whether America still believes what it once taught the world.

— Chris
